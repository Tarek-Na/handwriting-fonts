# Draw Arabic as strokes, join by construction

Build a **stroke-native pipeline**, not a better image generator. In it, the photographed letters are turned into ordered pen centrelines. A small few-shot trajectory model, in the style of SDT, fills in the missing positional forms as centrelines, with fixed entry and exit join points. Each glyph is then stroked with the writer's measured pen and compiled into an OpenType font whose `curs` anchors come straight from the stroke endpoints. The research supports this choice for four reasons. First, no deep model that generates Arabic *online strokes* in a writer's style was found, and no few-shot font paper has been evaluated on Arabic contextual forms. Second, no consumer handwriting-to-font product was found that emits Arabic positional forms. Third, the hardest Arabic font problem is joining, and that is solved by construction when glyphs are strokes but is fragile when they are traced rasters. Fourth, every trajectory model outputs a centreline, which is exactly the representation the project's pen redraw and centreline F1 already use. Arabic will not escape the Latin limits. Per-glyph writer style is weakly identifiable (SDT's English writer classifier reached 20.57%; our own second copy scores only ~0.50 F1), so the novelty and the value sit in the *system*: writer-measured strokes, rasm-and-dots composition from the writer's own letters, and generated joins that actually connect. Model cleverness is secondary. The recommended order is a no-ML Arabic stroke font first, then composition, then a Latin bridge test of the SDT-style generator on our existing scores, then an Arabic generator trained on AHAWP letters recovered into strokes. That last dataset is the one public set in which 82 writers each write all 65 positional forms ten times. All of it fits one 24 GB L4.

## Trajectory models: SDT is the only one that fits, and it is weakest on Latin

Most of the 2020-2026 online-handwriting literature is Chinese and line-level, and little of it is usable here. **SDT (CVPR 2023)** is the one documented, open, character-level generator that takes *offline images* as style input. Its style encoder is a ResNet18 plus a 2-layer Transformer over **K = 15 reference images at 64×64**, split by two contrastive heads (WriterNCE, GlyphNCE) into writer-level and character-level style. Its decoder is an autoregressive Transformer that emits **a 20-component bivariate Gaussian mixture over pen offsets plus three pen states**. It trained on **a single 24 GB RTX 3090** (138k content-encoder iterations, then 148k full-model iterations at batch 128; wall-clock time not reported). The code is **MIT-licensed** and includes an `English_CASIA.yml` config for the 52 Latin letters ([SDT paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.14736); [repo](https://github.com/dailenson/SDT)). On Chinese it reached a Style Score of 94.50 against 72.37 for WriteLikeYou-v2. Its English numbers show the limit we already know: the writer identifier used to score style reached **20.57% on English, against 99.98% on Chinese**, so no English Style Score is even reported ([SDT paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.14736)). That matches our SD19 ceiling, where a writer's own second copy is attributed only 38% of the time. Latin and Arabic letters carry little writer signal per glyph, whatever the model.

The rest of the field is less usable. **DiffInk** (2025) is the strongest recent result: an InkVAE with 8× temporal compression plus a 16-layer latent DiT, 77.38% line-level Style against SDT's 50.51%. But it is Chinese and line-level, takes *online* references, trained on 4× A6000 for about four days, and its code "will be made publicly available" ([DiffInk](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23624v1)). **OLHWG** (ICLR 2025), a 1D U-Net diffusion denoiser over glyph trajectories, has code but no licence, no weights, and a README admitting lost files ([repo](https://github.com/singularityrms/OLHWG)). **Kotani et al.'s DSD** (ECCV 2020) is the main Latin few-shot trajectory model, but it carries a non-commercial licence, its BRUSH data is research-only, and its weights are "not currently available" ([repo](https://github.com/brownvc/decoupled-style-descriptors)). **Luhman & Luhman (2020)** shows a stroke diffusion model conditioned on offline style images, without auxiliary losses, on IAM ([arXiv 2011.06704](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06704v1)). It is the second architecture that accepts photographs as style. SDT's own successors from the same group, One-DM and DiffBrush, generate *images*, not trajectories ([SDT repo](https://github.com/dailenson/SDT)).

One property of this family matters more than any benchmark. Our model collapses to the average letter because it regresses pixels under L1 + Dice. SDT and DiffInk instead model a *distribution* over the next pen move (GMM likelihood, sampled) or denoise trajectories (diffusion). Both draw one plausible stroke rather than the mean of all plausible strokes. This is a reasoned argument, not a measurement. **No paper compares trajectory and image generators on the same writers** ([SDT paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.14736)). The cost of the family is also clear: online models cannot reproduce stroke width or ink ([One-DM](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2024/papers_ECCV/papers/07598.pdf)). That is not a loss for us. The pen redraw already showed that weight should be measured and imposed, not generated.

## Arabic has old concatenation prior art, image-only deep models, and thin stroke data

Arabic handwriting synthesis splits into two lines, and neither produces a font. The **KFUPM line** (Elarian et al., Pattern Recognition 2015, and a 2014 thesis) builds words from per-writer positional-form glyphs joined by "extended glyphs" (the writer's own connecting tail is kept) or "synthetic extensions" (a generated joining stroke) ([KFUPM PURE](https://pure.kfupm.edu.sa/en/publications/an-arabic-handwriting-synthesis-system/); [thesis](https://eprints.kfupm.edu.sa/id/eprint/139204/)). A family of US patents from the same line covers connection-point analysis, reports connection-point localisation errors of **1.64% (right) and 2.12% (left)**, and names **personalised fonts** as an application ([US10083362](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10083362); [US10242276](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10242276)). That is prior art to respect. The concatenation-plus-connection-point idea is not ours to claim, and a commercial release should have the patents reviewed. Dinges et al. synthesised Arabic pages with Active Shape Models trained on 28,046 *online* samples ([PMC4534626](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4534626/)). The deep line is entirely image-based: a ScrabbleGAN fork for IFN/ENIT augmentation ([PeerJ CS 861](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-861/)), DiffusionRec for Urdu Nastaliq words ([NUST](https://tukl.seecs.nust.edu.pk/members/projects/conference/DiffusionRec-Recognition-Guided-Diffusion-for-Content-Aware-Urdu-Handwriting-Generation.pdf)), and **Arabic-One-DM**, which adapts One-DM to Arabic word images from 62,437 images by 1,417 writers. Arabic-One-DM handles positional forms only on the *content* side, by shaping GNU Unifont glyphs with a heuristic. It reports no quantitative results, and its repo carries no licence ([repo](https://github.com/7abushahla/Arabic-One-DM)). Mainstream few-shot font models (LF-Font, MX-Font, DA-Font) were not evaluated on Arabic, and a 2024 multilingual one-shot font paper lists Arabic as future work ([arXiv 2412.11342](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11342)). **No neural Arabic online-stroke generator was found.**

The data decides what can be trained. The table lists the sets that matter for a stroke model, with their access status as checked on 14 November 2026.

| Dataset | Type | Writers / size | Positional forms per writer | Licence | Available today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Calliar** | online JSON strokes | 2,500 sentences, 40k+ strokes, labels at stroke/char/word level | calligraphy, not everyday hands; writer count not stated | MIT | yes, GitHub ([repo](https://github.com/ARBML/Calliar)) |

The striking fact is that the best Arabic set for this task is **offline**. AHAWP gives, for each of 82 writers, every positional form written ten times, with filenames that encode the writer, letter and form ([PMC8866147](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866147/)). That is exactly the structure few-shot training needs: hold out forms, condition on the rest. The ten repeats also give, for free, the **own-second-copy ceiling** that we had to construct for SD19. The downloadable online sets are either calligraphy (Calliar) or unsegmented words (ADAB). So the plan has to turn AHAWP into strokes, which is the same problem as turning the user's photographs into strokes.

## Isolated letters can be recovered by rule, which keeps training and input in one domain

Learned stroke recovery exists, but it does not cover Arabic. **InkSight** is the only learned photo-to-ink model with usable weights: Small-p, about 340M parameters, **Apache-2.0**, with a Colab notebook. Raters judged **87% of its HierText outputs valid tracings**, and adding its derendered ink to real ink cut IAMOnDB recognition CER from 6.1 to 4.5. But its training data does not mention Arabic, its authors list untrained scripts as a failure mode, and an online recogniser reads its ink at 0.37-0.46 accuracy against 0.74 for human ink ([arXiv 2402.05804](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05804v4); [repo](https://github.com/google-research/inksight)). The Arabic recovery work from the REGIM/Sfax group (a stacked denoising autoencoder plus BGRU with attention, on LMCA) has no released code and only an abstract that claims "highly competitive results" ([MTAP 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11042-023-16499-z)). The 2026 diffusion and stroke-instance methods are Chinese-trained, and their transfer to Latin works only "to some extent" ([arXiv 2607.03422](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03422)).

For *isolated* letters, the classical method is enough, and it has a structural advantage. A letter's skeleton graph has a handful of edges and at most a few junctions. So the combinatorial blow-up that defeats search on words ([arXiv 1905.06749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06749)) does not occur, and every Euler-path ordering can be enumerated and scored by smoothness, the established "smoothest Euler path" formulation ([survey](https://typeset.io/pdf/techniques-for-static-handwriting-trajectory-recovery-a-4t0al45bdg.pdf)). The letter's identity is also known from its slot, so the method can be **template-guided**. Match the skeleton to a per-letter, per-form ductus template (start point, direction, loops counter-clockwise from the top), draw the main body first, then dots and hamza by rule. The stubborn cases will be closed loops (ه م و ف ق ص), retraced tails on ر and و, and split junctions, and the template resolves the first two. This is an engineering inference; no published system does it for Arabic. The same module processes AHAWP scans for training and the user's photographs at test time. Both are then re-rendered at one thin pen for the style encoder. This avoids SDT's hidden domain gap, since its style images were rendered from clean online ink, and it is the "pen-normalised domain" the Colab record already names as its next training candidate. InkSight should get one cheap comparison run on Arabic letters. It should not be a dependency. Evaluate recovery against tablet captures from the owner with LDTW or direction-and-order agreement, not plain DTW, which is biased by point count ([PEN-Net](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.15879)).

## OpenType needs 148 glyphs, and joins are where stroke output pays off

The font side is well specified, and most of it is already in the repo. The shaper decides each letter's joining position from Unicode data. The font supplies `isol/init/medi/fina` single substitutions, a required lam-alef ligature under `rlig`, and optionally `calt`, `curs`, `mark` and `mkmk`, applied in that order ([Microsoft Arabic guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/script-development/arabic)). Basic Arabic has **24 dual-joining letters, 11 right-joining letters and hamza** ([ArabicShaping.txt](https://www.unicode.org/Public/UCD/latest/ucd/ArabicShaping.txt)). Counting four forms for the dual-joining letters, two for the right-joining ones, eight lam-alef glyphs, tatweel, Arabic-Indic digits and the required controls (ZWNJ, ZWJ, LRM, RLM, dotted circle U+25CC) gives **about 148 glyphs**. Google's `GF_Arabic_Core`, at about 383 names, is the fuller published target ([glyphsets](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/googlefonts/glyphsets/main/data/results/txt/nice-names/GF_Arabic_Core.txt)). The project's `src/hfont/fontbuild/arabic.py` already compiles `init/medi/fina` and `rlig` lam-alef with feaLib. It lacks `curs`, GDEF and `mark`, `calt` alternates, tatweel, digits, Arabic punctuation and the zero-width controls. The controls, punctuation and tatweel cost almost nothing and should come first.

Stroke output is what makes the remaining parts tractable. **OpenType renders only closed contours**, and COLR does not support open ones ([FontCreator](https://www.high-logic.com/fontcreator/manual15/fc_single-line-fonts.html)). So each centreline is expanded with `skia-pathops` (`Path.stroke` with round caps and joins, then `simplify`) and drawn into a fontTools pen ([skia-pathops](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fonttools/skia-pathops/main/src/python/pathops/_pathops.pyx)). The join is the crux. Type designers fix the connecting stroke to one height and thickness, with the exit at x = 0 and the entry on the right sidebearing ([Glyphs Arabic tutorial](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/arabic)). `curs` (GPOS type 3) then snaps each glyph's exit to the next glyph's entry, and with the `RightToLeft` flag the last glyph sits on the baseline ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/script-development/arabic); [Glyphs forum](https://forum.glyphsapp.com/t/righttoleft-lookup-flag/15678)). With traced rasters, join points have to be guessed from where an ink blob crosses the glyph edge. With strokes they are **the first and last points of the connecting stroke**, known exactly. So the pipeline should retarget the last 5-10% of each connecting stroke to a canonical join height with a horizontal tangent, stroke every join at one width with a butt cap overlapping by a few units, and write the endpoints as `curs` anchors. `curs` cannot fix a mismatch in tangent or thickness, so those constraints belong in generation: the model is conditioned on the positional form and given its entry and exit as boundary conditions. Tatweel doubles as a pure join segment and as the join test glyph. Lam-alef always resolves to `lam.init|lam.medi + alef.fina`, so two `rlig` rules per alef variant cover every case. For variety, deterministic `calt` cycling over `.alt1` forms works in every engine, whereas `rand` is barely supported ([HarfBuzz list](https://lists.freedesktop.org/archives/harfbuzz/2020-February/007475.html)). Every alternate must keep the same join points. Tests need no GUI. Shape `بيت`, `لا`, `سلام` and a ZWNJ case with `uharfbuzz`, assert the positional glyph names, and assert that exit plus position equals entry for each joined pair ([uharfbuzz](https://github.com/harfbuzz/uharfbuzz)).

Dots deserve a deliberate decision. Arabic's 28 letters collapse onto roughly **17-19 dotless skeletons (rasm)** plus dot marks, the way Glyphs builds `teh-ar` from `behDotless-ar` and two dots ([Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/arabic)). For a handwriting font, the better route is to bake dots into each glyph as strokes drawn after the body, in the writer's dot shape measured from their letters, rather than to use `mark` anchors. Baked dots are simpler and look less mechanical. The rasm grouping is used for *composition*, not for encoding.

## Ranked plan: five steps that fit one L4

The ranking follows the Latin lesson. Measured, imposed properties (size, pen, slant, joins) beat learned shape. The writer's own strokes beat any generator. A generator earns its place only on the letters nothing else can supply, and only if it beats the average letter on the held-out score.

| Rank | Build | Compute | Why here | Move on when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Stroke font from a full positional sheet.** About 120 cells (or words segmented into forms). Template-guided skeleton-to-stroke recovery, join retargeting, pen re-stroke, `curs` anchors from endpoints, `calt` alternates from a second copy, plus the cheap glyphs (controls, digits, punctuation, tatweel). | CPU | Gives the novel product with no model: an Arabic handwriting font whose joins connect. No consumer tool found does this ([Calligraphr features](https://www.calligraphr.com/features)). | uharfbuzz join-continuity tests pass on a test corpus; the owner's own Arabic sheet reads as connected text |
| A2 | **Rasm composition.** Fill missing letters and forms from the writer's own skeletons (ب→ت ث ن ي and their forms, ج→ح خ, د→ذ, ر→ز, س→ش, ص→ض, ط→ظ, ع→غ, hamza forms = base + hamza mark), and fill missing positional forms by grafting the writer's join strokes onto the body. | CPU | Shrinks the sheet to about one cell per rasm group and form. The writer's real strokes cover most glyphs, which is HandwritingAgent's lesson ([arXiv 2606.18788](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18788)). | Leave-one-out composed glyphs beat the average letter on centreline F1 against AHAWP held-out writers |
| A3 | **Latin bridge test of the stroke generator.** SDT with a letter token instead of a content image (our model already ignores the content glyph), trained on CASIA online Latin (1,020 writers), with style input re-rendered at one thin pen from recovered strokes. | L4, days | Same architecture as A4, scored on the scale we already trust: own second copy ~0.50, average letter ~0.40, other writer ~0.33, current best 0.40-0.42. The ten photographed writers stay test-only. | Clearly above 0.42 on the photographed writers. If not, the generator stays a filler for A4 and the product rests on A1-A2. |
| A4 | **Arabic stroke generator for missing forms.** SDT-style encoder and GMM decoder, conditioned on (letter, positional form, entry/exit points). Trained on AHAWP recovered to strokes (82 writers, 65 forms × 10), with Calliar and ADAB as a stroke-shape prior, warm-started from A3. Hold out AHAWP writers; score against their own repeats as the ceiling. | L4, days | Fills forms the writer did not write and no donor covers. Few-shot completion of Arabic positional forms as strokes is unpublished. | Beats A2's composed glyphs on held-out forms |
| A5 | **Trajectory diffusion** (OLHWG-style 1D U-Net over point sequences) in place of the autoregressive decoder. | L4, longer | Only if A4's samples are unstable or average. Diffusion leads in third-party tables ([DiffInk](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23624v1)). | Only if A4 stalls |

On compute, an L4 has the same 24 GB as SDT's documented 3090. Because SDT's wall-clock time is unreported, each training step should be budgeted in short warm-started arms, as in the November runs. A4's main risk is **82 writers**, against SDT's 1,020. That is why A3 pretrains on CASIA Latin and why the Arabic model conditions on so much fixed structure (form, joins, rasm). Collecting more Arabic writers with consent is the cheapest way to enlarge it. Three directions should be avoided. Arabic-One-DM and the other word-image diffusers produce rasters with no licence and no join control. Per-writer LoRA was already refuted on Latin. Vector-font LLMs carry a typeset prior. InkSight gets one comparison run, nothing more.

What would be new, stated carefully: no product found ships handwritten Arabic positional forms, though Calligraphr's Arabic templates were not tested directly. No published model generates Arabic handwriting as strokes, and none completes missing positional forms few-shot. No published work enforces join constraints on ML-generated Arabic glyphs. The concatenation-with-connection-points idea itself is KFUPM prior art and patented, so the claim is the learned, stroke-level, font-compiled version, not the decomposition.

## Conclusion

The move to Arabic reverses which part of the system is hard. On Latin, the font was easy and the letters were the research problem, and the evidence now says per-letter style has a low ceiling for every method. On Arabic, a correct font is itself unsolved for handwriting. Joins, positional forms and lam-alef are where every tool found falls short, and they are geometric problems that a stroke representation answers exactly while a raster representation only approximates them. That makes the stroke route the right architecture whether or not the generator ever beats the average letter. A1 and A2 deliver a defensible, unique product on a CPU, and A3 tells cheaply whether learned completion adds anything before Arabic training money is spent.

The quieter finding is that the project's most useful Arabic asset is an offline dataset plus a rule-based recovery module, not an online corpus. Recovering AHAWP and the user's photographs with the same template-guided tracer, and rendering both at one thin pen, removes the photo-versus-tablet gap that every published trajectory model leaves open. AHAWP's ten repeats per form also give Arabic the own-second-copy ceiling from the start. Arabic evaluation can therefore begin where Latin evaluation ended: with a ceiling, a floor and a weight-fair score, set before any model is trained.
