# Teach the generator to copy the hand

The best-supported fix for style collapse is not a new model family. It is to make the style path able to move strokes, and to train it so that it has to. The local-style literature from 2022 to 2026 (FS-Font, VQ-Font, DA-Font, DRG-Font) gets its largest style gains from three things: dense attention from each output location to the reference glyphs, a self-reconstruction branch that forces that attention to copy local detail, and choosing, for each target letter, the references that are structurally related to it. Our model has a weak version of the first and none of the other two. Diffusion (FontDiffuser, Diff-Font, IF-Font, HFH-Font) is the right second step, because its mode-seeking objective attacks the "average letter" that our L1 + Dice loss rewards. But no published diffusion font model has been tested on Latin handwriting, and training one costs days on a single 24 GB card. Per-writer LoRA or DreamBooth is the wrong direction: published evidence agrees with our 0.485 → 0.382 result that 10-30 examples get memorised rather than generalised. Two cheap moves matter regardless of model. First, NIST SD19's 1995 first edition ships per-writer label files, so real handwriting can be added to training without image matching. Second, composing missing letters from the writer's own strokes puts real handwriting into roughly a third of the generated glyphs on CPU today. A 2026 glyph-bank system shows that retrieval plus adaptation can match generative handwriting models. The ranked plan at the end puts evaluation, SD19 labels, composition and the gate-3 test on the CPU now. On the GPU, in order: higher-resolution reference attention, self-reconstruction, SD19 fine-tuning, reference selection, shape and adversarial losses, then a Latin diffusion model.

## Pooled style and coarse attention explain the collapse; reference count does not

The literature describes our failure closely. FS-Font (CVPR 2022) argues that methods which disentangle content and style "globally or component-wisely" miss style that is "distributed over spatial locations". When its attention was replaced by averaged reference features, LPIPS rose from 0.1627 to 0.1822 and SSIM fell from 0.642 to 0.621 ([ar5iv 2205.09965](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.09965)). VQ-Font's ablation is sharper still. Removing local style attention moved FID from 110.1 to 145.0 and user preference from **72.4% to 7.3%** ([ar5iv 2309.00827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.00827)). Our decoder receives style only through AdaIN (per-channel scale and shift from a pooled code), plus attention at an 8×8 bottleneck over 4×4 reference keys. At that resolution one attention cell covers 16-32 px of a 128-px glyph, which is too coarse to place a loop, a terminal or a second stroke on a `k`. The project's measurements show the decoder ignores both the content font and the skips. So the shape it draws is a learned average letter per character, with the writer's weight and slant applied. Weight and slant are the information channel statistics can carry.

The literature also makes a counter-intuitive point: we are not short of references. Published few-shot systems condition on 1 to 8 glyphs: VQ-Font uses 3 by default with diminishing returns up to 8 ([ar5iv 2309.00827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.00827)), DA-Font 4 ([arXiv 2509.16632](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16632v1)), and DRG-Font one reference chosen from ten ([arXiv 2604.13797](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13797v1)). Our ~30 letters are 4-30 times more. The bottleneck is a path that cannot use them, not the amount of reference data.

Our model already takes letter identity from the content glyph and nothing else, which is in effect the token-content design of Diff-Font ([arXiv 2212.05895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.05895v3)) and IF-Font. That has a consequence for which methods fit. The deformation family warps the content glyph's features. This covers DG-Font's deformable skips ([arXiv 2104.03064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.03064)), FontDiffuser's reference-structure offsets ([ar5iv 2312.12142](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.12142)), CF-Font's fusion of basis fonts ([arXiv 2303.14017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14017)) and NTF's transformation fields ([CVPR 2023](https://cvpr2023.thecvf.com/virtual/2023/poster/21627)). These methods operate on a signal our decoder already discards, and that signal is a typeset skeleton anyway. Shape has to come from the reference path. IF-Font removes the source glyph entirely and claims its biggest gains "when the target styles differ significantly from the training font styles" ([mlanthology](https://mlanthology.org/neurips/2024/chen2024neurips-iffont)). That supports the same conclusion.

One caveat covers everything below. **Almost every method was trained and scored on Chinese fonts**, all numbers are the authors' own, and no paper measures whether a writer's letter *construction* survives (stroke count, a top bar on `i`). The only recent Latin numbers for a local-style GAN come from DRG-Font, on 811 typeset fonts at 64 px ([arXiv 2604.13797](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13797v1)). Every claim of fit below is a hypothesis to test on our writers, not a result.

## Attention, self-reconstruction and reference selection have the strongest shape evidence

### Self-reconstruction, not the attention block, carried the largest ablation drop

FS-Font's Style Aggregation Module is 8-head cross-attention: content positions are the queries, reference features the keys and values, and the output is concatenated back into the content features before decoding. Its **self-reconstruction branch** feeds the ground-truth target glyph in as its own reference, with shared weights and L1 plus adversarial loss. Removing that branch hurt more than removing anything else: SSIM fell from 0.642 to 0.596 and L1 rose from 0.106 to 0.127. Removing attention gave 0.621, removing reference selection 0.624, and removing all three 0.584. In its user study FS-Font won 67.1% of style votes against at most 13.3% for LF-Font, MX-Font and DG-Font ([ar5iv 2205.09965](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.09965)).

The mechanism explains our symptom. A model that has learned to draw the average letter has never been forced to copy local appearance from a reference, so its attention has no reason to become sharp. The self-reconstruction branch provides that pressure. It costs one extra forward pass, and a target is never among its own references at test time. Combined with attention at 16×16 and 32×32 (the style encoder already produces these maps), it is the most direct test of whether the style path can move strokes. No paper ablates multi-scale attention for fonts as such, so the resolution change itself is our own bet. It is cheap to warm-start because `ReferenceAttention` initialises as an identity.

### Reference selection is label-free for Latin

FS-Font's greedy selection uses a Chinese component table, which Latin does not have. Two label-free variants carry over. VQ-Font weights each reference by the normalised cross-correlation between its content features and the target's ([ar5iv 2309.00827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.00827)). DRG-Font skeletonises the glyphs, splits them into strokes, and ranks candidates by stroke length, curvature and an orientation histogram. It is the only method here with Latin results: SSIM 0.739 vs 0.713 for DA-Font and 53.42% user preference, on a single RTX 4080 ([arXiv 2604.13797](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13797v1)). In our setting this means letting `n` draw mainly on `h`, `m` and `u`, and `q` on `g`, `d` and `a`, instead of averaging over all 30 letters.

### Shape losses and multi-task discriminators counter the average letter

DA-Font adds a corner-consistency loss (Shi-Tomasi corners matched in both directions) and an elastic-mesh patch loss, plus a multi-task discriminator. It reports SSIM 0.7352 against MX-Font's 0.6966 and FID 54.19 against VQ-Font's 58.26, beating FontDiffuser and IF-Font as well ([arXiv 2509.16632](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16632v1)). CF-Font's projected character loss compares 1-D projections as distributions and is said to emphasise "global character shape" over L1/L2 ([arXiv 2303.14017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14017)). Both are architecture-independent and penalise exactly the hedged geometry our L1 + Dice rewards. They suit the adversarial arm the project already has, since that discriminator has character and font heads. Its earlier failure at weight 0.1 says little about these losses.

### Training cost fits one GPU if we warm-start

Published GAN budgets are 500K-600K iterations at batch 8-48 at 128 px. FS-Font used batch 32 for 500K ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.09965)), VQ-Font batch 48 for 500K ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.00827)) and DA-Font batch 8 for 600K ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16632v1)). Parameter counts and wall-clock times are not reported. Training from scratch would take days per run. Borrowing the modules into our 32M model and warm-starting 10K-step arms from the 40K checkpoint keeps each experiment to a single session. Code exists for VQ-Font ([GitHub](https://github.com/awei669/VQ-Font)), DA-Font ([GitHub](https://github.com/wrchen2001/DA-Font)), CF-Font ([GitHub](https://github.com/wangchi95/CF-Font)) and the MIT-licensed clovaai collection (FUNIT, DM-Font, LF-Font, MX-Font), which accepts rendered image folders and names handwriting as the use case ([GitHub](https://github.com/clovaai/fewshot-font-generation)). No code was found for FS-Font or DRG-Font, but both modules are small enough to reimplement.

## Diffusion fixes the objective, but nobody has shown it on Latin hands

Diffusion replaces the loss that produces the average letter. FontDiffuser (AAAI 2024) injects style by cross-attention (style as keys and values) and adds a phase-2 style-contrastive refinement. Against MX-Font it reports FID 8.54 vs 10.77 on unseen fonts and characters, and 13.12 vs 16.40 on hard characters ([ar5iv 2312.12142](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.12142)). Its own ablation shows FID getting *worse* (8.12 → 8.54) as modules were added while SSIM and LPIPS improved. That is a warning that these metrics do not track style. Diff-Font reports FID 27.30 against 35.20-43.44 for the GANs and admits that "uncommon styles still fail" ([ar5iv 2212.05895](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.05895)). HFH-Font distils sampling to **one step** and outputs 1024 px glyphs ready for vectorising ([arXiv 2410.06488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06488)). GRIF-DM is a rare Latin diffusion font model, but it conditions on impression keywords, not on a writer ([ar5iv 2408.07259](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2408.07259)). None of these reports results on handwritten Latin glyphs.

Compute is within reach but slow. FontDiffuser trained on **one RTX 3090** at 96 px, batch 16, for 440K + 30K steps, and samples in 20 DPM-Solver++ steps with CFG 7.5 ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.12142)). A 128-px version has about 1.8× the pixels, which fits 24 GB and probably needs mixed precision on 12 GB. A full 75-glyph font is about 3,000 UNet evaluations, which can be batched. The code and weights are public, but under a non-commercial research licence. The Chinese weights do not help with Latin, so the training code would be the reusable part ([GitHub](https://github.com/yeungchenwa/FontDiffuser)). The design to borrow is Diff-Font's or IF-Font's: a letter token for identity, the writer's letters via cross-attention, and no source glyph. The data shows our model already works this way.

Handwriting-generation (HTG) diffusion models are the only diffusion family trained on idiosyncratic hands. One-DM imitates a writer from one reference via high-frequency features and a contrastive loss. It reports IAM FID 89.47 against 98.10 for WordStylist and CER 11.69% against 26.98% for VATr, but its authors say **single-letter references perform poorly** and recommend more than four letters. It was trained on 4× RTX 3090 for 700 epochs ([arXiv 2409.04004](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04004v2)). DiffusionPen is a 5-shot latent model on IAM with weights on Hugging Face ([GitHub](https://github.com/koninik/DiffusionPen)). Both are MIT-licensed. They take word images in and produce word images out, so using them means tiling a writer's isolated glyphs into pseudo-words and cropping single characters. That workaround is untested. Their style encoders could still be useful as priors or evaluators.

Large text-to-image models have one pattern that works: generate the **whole alphabet as one atlas**, so all letters share one sampling pass. Ref2Font, a FLUX.2 Klein 9B LoRA, turns an "Aa" sample into a 1280×1280 Latin/Cyrillic atlas with a TTF script ([Civitai](https://civitai.com/models/2361340/ref2font-flux2-klein-9b-fonts-lora)). Generating letters one at a time gives no consistency across the alphabet ([TypeDrawers](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/4665/alphabetmagic-my-first-ai-experiment/p12)). A 9B model needs about 18 GB for bf16 weights alone, so it is inference-only on a 24 GB card and needs quantisation on 12 GB. Our ~30 letters could be placed into their atlas cells and the rest inpainted. No one has measured fidelity to idiosyncratic hands, and the pretraining prior is typeset text. Scene-text models (GlyphControl, AnyText, TextDiffuser) condition on a rendered glyph, which repeats our content-font setup, and they lose fine detail on thin strokes ([arXiv 2502.10999](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10999)). They do not fit.

### Parameter-efficient adaptation: adapt the encoder across writers, not the model per writer

Our test-time result has outside support. With 10-30 images, DreamBooth "essentially memorizes" its training set, and LoRA only reduces the copying ([arXiv 2510.09475](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09475)). The 2025 font work avoids per-font tuning altogether. FontAdapter trains an IP-Adapter-style reference branch on SD3 across about 1,500 fonts. It customises in 11 seconds on an RTX 3090 with no per-font fine-tuning, where a per-font SD3 LoRA takes about 40 minutes on four GPUs ([arXiv 2506.05843](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05843v1)). With one example per letter, a per-writer tuner learns "this image is this letter", not the writer's rules of construction. The parameter-efficient lesson for us is to train a reference adapter across thousands of *real writers* (SD19) so that it works zero-shot. If per-writer tuning is ever revisited, the conditions to try are low rank, a few steps, rendered fonts as the prior-preservation class, and early stopping on held-out letters. No glyph-level evidence for any of them was found.

## Strokes, retrieval and composition keep shape that raster models lose

HandwritingAgent (June 2026) segments the sample into a glyph bank and reuses real exemplars. It fills missing characters from a backbone bank adapted to the writer's slant, proportions, curvature and spacing, with no style training. On IAM words it reports the best SSIM (0.67), FID (88.08) and HWD (1.33) among the methods compared, though Emuru beats it on lines (FID 19.69 vs 70.92) ([arXiv 2606.18788](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18788)). It relies on Gemini 3.1 Pro, so we would not reproduce it as published. Its lesson transfers: **retrieval plus geometric adaptation is a state-of-the-art-competitive imitator**, and composition guarantees the writer's own strokes. Latin lowercase shares a lot: `n` gives `h`, `m`, `u` and `r`; `a` plus an ascender gives `d`; `o`, `c` and `e` share a bowl; `b`↔`d` and `p`↔`q` are mirrors with adjustment. This is already item 5.5 in `instruction.md`. The published precedents are skeleton-based stroke reuse for Chinese ([arXiv 1701.05703](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05703v1.pdf)) and rule-based reuse for Gujarati ([arXiv 2404.03277](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03277v1)). No Latin evaluation exists. Letters with no donor, such as `k`, `s`, `x`, `z` and `g`, still need the generator. A nearest-handwriting-font retrieval baseline (closest Google Font by style embedding, rescaled to the writer's metrics) is a cheap floor that any generator must beat.

Pen-trajectory models are the one family with a published **character-level few-shot writer** setting. SDT (CVPR 2023) separates writer-level from character-level style with two contrastive heads ([CVF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/html/Dai_Disentangling_Writer_and_Character_Styles_for_Handwriting_Generation_CVPR_2023_paper.html)). DSD imitates Latin writers from a few online samples on BRUSH ([ar5iv 2008.11354](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2008.11354)). Both need online ink, but our input is isolated letters of one to three strokes, the easiest case for recovering pen paths. InkSight's VLM-based derendering produced 87% valid tracings on HierText ([arXiv 2402.05804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05804)), and skeleton tracing is probably enough for boxed letters. This route models geometry directly, so it should keep topology. It is also the longest pipeline (photo → ink → Latin stroke model trained on BRUSH or CASIA → re-render with the writer's pen → outline), and nobody has run it end to end on photographed Latin glyphs. It belongs after diffusion.

Vector-font generators learn typeface outline priors from Google Fonts and Envato. That covers DeepVecFont-v2 ([arXiv 2303.14585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14585)), VecFusion ([CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Thamizharasan_VecFusion_Vector_Font_Generation_with_Diffusion_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)) and the 2026 MLLM VecGlypher ([arXiv 2602.21461](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21461)). None was tested on personal handwriting, and their typographic regularisation would probably make our collapse worse. Their place is downstream, if anywhere. Tracing the raster output, or re-stroking skeletons with the writer's measured width profile, gives consistent vectors for real, composed and generated glyphs alike.

## SD19's first edition removes the label obstacle

Our data is the root cause that models cannot fix: ~3,000 mostly typeset Google Fonts teach no idiosyncrasy. NIST SD19 is still the only large offline Latin set in which thousands of writers each print the full `a-z` and `A-Z` once, in boxes, with writer IDs. Each form has one lowercase field and one uppercase field in a random order chosen from 100 templates. Labels were checked twice by different people, with about 0.1% residual error. In total there are 81,096 lowercase and 81,463 uppercase letters ([1st ed. guide](https://s3.amazonaws.com/nist-srd/SD19/1stEditionUserGuide.pdf)). **`instruction.md` says the by_write images carry no labels; that holds only for the 2nd edition.** The 1st-edition guide states that "all .mis files on Special Database 19 have an associated .cls file", including by_write. The NIST page still offers `1stEdition1995.zip` next to the 2nd-edition zips ([NIST SD19](https://www.nist.gov/srd/nist-special-database-19)). The 2nd edition names each PNG after its `.mis` file plus a zero-based index (`d0000_14_00000.png` …), so 1st-edition labels should map one to one onto the PNGs already downloaded ([2nd ed. guide](https://s3.amazonaws.com/nist-srd/SD19/sd19_users_guide_edition_2.pdf)). This is unverified. Check it on one writer first, and keep hash-matching against by_field as a cross-check.

Two properties must be checked before SD19 enters the cache. First, the 1st edition includes `normmis`, a size-normalisation tool, which suggests the stored rasters are not normalised. Neither guide documents where a glyph sits in its 128×128 raster, though, so compare bounding-box centroids of `g`, `y`, `p` against `x`, `o` within each writer. Second, roughly 22-23 of each 26-letter field survive segmentation (81,463 uppercase letters over about 3,600 writers), so writers will have gaps. The writers are 1990s Census staff and Bethesda students, boxed and binary, with real but constrained idiosyncrasy. We found no licence text beyond NIST's generic copyright link.

Other sources are weaker for glyph targets. EMNIST re-centres every glyph to 28×28 and drops writer IDs ([arXiv 1702.05373](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05373v1)). CASIA-HWDB/OLHWDB includes the 52 Latin letters for **1,020 writers**, online and offline, by application ([CASIA](https://nlpr.ia.ac.cn/databases/handwriting/home.html)). That makes it the best second source, and the natural training set for a stroke model. DeepWriting has character-segmented online ink from 294 writers, with a median of 1,349 characters each ([ar5iv 1801.08379](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1801.08379)). BRUSH has 170 online writers and is non-commercial ([GitHub](https://github.com/brownvc/decoupled-style-descriptors)). IAM and CVL are word-level, and alignment-based character crops "often fail" at the boundaries ([arXiv 2307.00664](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.00664v1)). They are better used to train a writer-style encoder or evaluator than as glyph targets.

## Measure "same hand" directly, and design gate 3 with a floor and a ceiling

Pixel metrics reward the average letter. That is why leave-one-out clDice barely moves between very different models, and why FontDiffuser's FID and SSIM disagreed. The best automatic proxy for writer style is **HWD**, the distance between mean real and mean generated features from a VGG16 trained on fonts. On IAM it separates same from different authors with 0.7% overlap where FID has 27.1%, and it stabilises at about 100 samples where FID needs about 2,000. It works on word images at height 32 and has never been validated against human judgement ([arXiv 2310.20316](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.20316v1)). Writer-classifier accuracy is the other standard: WordStylist reaches 70.67% where the GAN baselines score under 5% ([arXiv 2303.16576](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16576)). For us, the cheapest version is a glyph-level writer-ID or retrieval network trained on SD19 writers, never on the ten test writers. Add it to the two metrics `instruction.md` already specifies: style sensitivity (swap writers' references) and cross-writer similarity (currently about 0.99).

Human tests in handwriting papers sit near chance. HWT had 100 participants label samples as real or forged after seeing the writer's real paragraph, and scored 48.1% accuracy, with real samples called fake half the time ([ar5iv 2104.03964](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.03964)). One-DM got 49.05% after showing 30 real samples ([arXiv 2409.04004](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04004v1)), and CONSTANT 53.8% from 28 raters ([WACV 2026 supp.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2026/supplemental/Le_CONSTANT_Towards_High-Quality_WACV_2026_supplemental.pdf)). HWT's confusion matrix exposes the problem: near 50% can mean the fakes are good, or that raters cannot tell hands apart at all. Gate 3 as written mixes two tests. Under "which is the real letter?" a perfect generator scores 50%, whereas "≥70% same hand" assumes a yes/no judgement. Pick one and anchor it. In the yes/no version, show the writer's real letters, then a probe that is a generated letter, a genuine held-out letter from the same writer (the ceiling) or a real letter from a different writer (the floor), and report all three rates. HYPE's protocol supplies the mechanics: 50% real, no time limit, randomised sides and order, error rate with bootstrap intervals ([arXiv 1904.01121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01121)). At 100 judgements a 70% rate has a 95% interval of about ±9 points, so separating 70% from 60% needs several hundred judgements per condition. Report inter-rater agreement too: even on simple perceptual 2AFC, humans agree only 73.9% of the time ([arXiv 1801.03924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.03924.pdf)). Isolated glyphs carry less writer information than words, so expect weaker discrimination than in these papers. The page shows real handwriting, so it must stay private and out of git.

## Ranked plan: four CPU tasks now, then a baseline and six GPU arms

The ordering follows one principle. Measure style before changing the model, then give the style path the ability to move strokes before giving it better data or a better objective. Without a shape-carrying path, AdaIN would turn SD19 into "the writer's weight on an average SD19 letter". Each GPU arm changes one variable, warm-starts from the previous winner, runs 10K steps, and is judged on thresholds fixed in advance.

**On CPU now**

| Rank | Task | Why first | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Add style sensitivity, cross-writer similarity and a held-out-letter shape check to `eval_writers.py`; set the GPU arms' pass thresholds now | clDice cannot rank the arms; thresholds must come before the runs | The current model scores about 0.99 cross-writer similarity, reproducibly |
| C2 | Download `1stEdition1995.zip`, map `.cls` labels onto by_write PNGs for one writer, cross-check against by_field hashes, measure glyph placement in the raster, write the cache converter | Unblocks the data arm; replaces the hash-matching plan | Label agreement on a test writer; per-writer coverage table |
| C3 | Composition at font assembly (donor table: `n`→`u`/`h`/`m`/`r`, `a`→`d`, `E`→`F`/`L`, `R`→`P`, `O`→`Q`, `6`→`9`), plus a nearest-handwriting-font baseline | Real strokes in about 15 of 45 glyphs whatever the model does; gives a floor | Leave-one-out on donor-able letters beats the model on all metrics |
| C4 | Build the private gate-3 page with floor and ceiling conditions, and fix the sample size (several hundred judgements per condition) | The human test is the final judge; protocol errors cost a round of raters | Pilot with one rater shows ceiling above floor |

CPU time also covers implementing and unit-testing the GPU arms' code: multi-scale `ReferenceAttention`, the self-reconstruction branch, reference weighting and the corner and projection losses. Each needs an identity-at-initialisation test and a few-step smoke run, so the GPU is never idle waiting for code.

**On the GPU, in order**

| Rank | Arm | Evidence | Move on if |
|---|---|---|---|
| G0 | Train the recommended recipe (attention + contrastive) to 40K | Best 10K arm (val IoU 0.571 vs 0.492) | The baseline for everything below |
| G1 | Reference attention at 16×16 and 32×32 | FS-Font and VQ-Font local-attention ablations | Style sensitivity rises and cross-writer similarity falls |
| G2 | Self-reconstruction branch (target as its own reference) | Largest single FS-Font ablation drop | Blind attempts follow writer constructions such as Bahaa's `k` |
| G3 | Fine-tune on SD19 + Google Fonts | Only source of thousands of real hands; the FontAdapter lesson | Gains on held-out writers, not only SD19 validation |
| G4 | Per-target reference weighting (VQ-Font correlation or DRG-Font stroke matching) | DRG-Font's Latin result; FS-Font selection ablation | Letters with related donors improve most |
| G5 | Corner and projection losses, and an adversarial weight well above 0.1 with the multi-task discriminator | DA-Font, CF-Font | Excess-ink hedge shrinks without breaking strokes |
| G6 | Latin diffusion model (FontDiffuser-style training code, letter token in place of a source glyph, SD19 + fonts, 128 px), or HFH-style distillation for speed | Mode-seeking objective; single-3090 precedent | Only if G1-G5 leave the shape metrics flat; budget days |

Exploratory side-runs need the GPU and are worth one session each, not a programme. One is Ref2Font atlas inpainting with the writer's letters fixed in their cells (inference only, 24 GB). Another is One-DM or DiffusionPen with pseudo-word references. The last is the stroke route via SDT trained on CASIA Latin letters. Three directions should be left alone: per-writer LoRA or DreamBooth, vector-font generators as shape models, and scene-text diffusion. The literature's memorisation evidence and our own measurement both argue against the first, and the font prior and the content-glyph design argue against the other two.

## Conclusion

Our measurements and the literature together change the question. We do not need a model that "captures style better" in the abstract. We need one whose shape-producing pathway reads the references at a resolution where letter construction exists, and which is trained so that copying from references pays. Content-side deformation, more references and per-writer tuning all fail that test for the same reason: they act on signals the decoder ignores, or on too little data to learn rules rather than images. Diffusion is the backstop, not the next step, because a better objective on a typeset-only corpus with a weak reference path would still draw well-formed typeset letters.

The largest unknown is not a model. No published method has been scored on whether a specific writer's Latin letter construction survives, so this project is building that evaluation for the field as much as for itself. That makes C1 and C4 the work most likely to decide the outcome. Composition (C3) also guarantees that a shipped font contains a substantial share of real handwriting even if every GPU arm fails. That is a credible minimum outcome, and the papers surveyed do not offer one.
